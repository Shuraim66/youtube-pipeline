## Opening hook (use this first)
Most "old money" doesn't stay old. Economists Priti Kalsi and Zachary Ward followed American families through census records from 1850 to 1940. Of the grandchildren of men in the top 1%, 93% did not reach the top 1% themselves. Wealth fades fast. So what do the families who keep it actually do differently? Here are nine habits, and the evidence behind each one.
- **Source:** Priti Kalsi and Zachary Ward, "The Gilded Age and Beyond: The Persistence of Elite Wealth in American History," NBER working paper, 2025 (as reported by InvestmentNews).
- Supporting fact (optional): in the same study, 72% of people in the top 1% had fallen out of it within ten years.

## Myth-check (after the hook, before habit 9)
You may have heard that 70% of wealthy families lose their money by the second generation, and 90% by the third. That figure is usually credited to a Williams Group study of 3,200 families, but family-wealth consultant James Grubman has challenged it, in a 2022 paper titled "There is no 70% rule." The real research, like the census study above, still shows wealth fading. Just don't trust a neat round number without checking where it came from.
- **Sources:** Williams Group study as cited by InvestmentNews; James Grubman, "There is no 70% rule" (2022), as cited by InvestmentNews.
- **Rule:** never state the 70% / 90% figures as fact. Only as "an often-quoted claim that has been challenged."

## The nine habits

### 9. They don't wait for an inheritance
- **Key fact:** a Federal Reserve analysis of 2016 survey data estimated how much of the top 10%'s wealth traces back to inheritances and gifts. Depending on the assumption about investment returns, it was about a quarter (25%) or about half (53%). Either way, a large share of wealth at the top was built, not handed down.
- **Source:** Federal Reserve, FEDS Notes, "How Does Intergenerational Wealth Transmission Affect Wealth Concentration?" (June 2018), using the Survey of Consumer Finances 2016.
- **Fix:** plan as if no inheritance is coming. Build your own base first.

### 8. They avoid expensive debt
- **Key fact:** in the third quarter of 2026, the average interest rate on US credit card accounts that were charged interest was 22.36%.
- **Source:** Federal Reserve, G.19 Consumer Credit release (November 2026).
- **Fix:** if you carry a card balance, paying it off is one of the highest "returns" you can get. Treat it as an emergency.

### 7. They don't buy status
- **Key fact:** in the research behind *The Millionaire Next Door*, nearly 37% of the millionaires surveyed said they had bought their most recent car used rather than new.
- **Source:** Thomas J. Stanley and William D. Danko, *The Millionaire Next Door* (1996).
- **Fix:** spend on what you value, not on what others will see.
- **Note for Claude Code:** this figure comes from a secondary summary of the book. Keep it, but name the book as the source and don't add any other figures from the book.

### 6. They keep a budget
- **Key fact:** in the same research, more than half of the millionaires said they stuck to a budget.
- **Source:** Stanley and Danko, *The Millionaire Next Door* (1996).
- **Fix:** a simple monthly plan beats a perfect one you never keep.

### 5. They automate saving
- **Key fact:** at one large US company, 401(k) participation among new employees was 37% when they had to sign up themselves. Under automatic enrolment it was about 86%.
- **Source:** Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea, "The Power of Suggestion," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2001.
- **Fix:** make saving the default. Move money on payday, before you can spend it.

### 4. They hold, they don't chase
- **Key fact:** Morningstar's 2025 "Mind the Gap" study found that over ten years, investors earned about 1.2 percentage points a year less than the funds they owned, losing roughly 15% of the funds' potential gains, mostly from buying and selling at the wrong times.
- **Source:** Morningstar, "Mind the Gap 2025" (August 2025).
- **Fix:** pick a simple long-term plan and stop reacting to the news.

### 3. They write things down: a will and a plan
- **Key fact:** in a 2025 survey of more than 2,500 American adults, only 24% said they had a will. The most common reason for not having one: they "just haven't gotten around to it" (43%).
- **Source:** Caring.com and YouGov, 2025 Wills and Estate Planning Survey.
- **Supporting fact:** even among wealthy Americans, a 2024 Bank of America survey of 1,007 high-net-worth respondents found 52% lacked a will, an advance healthcare directive or a durable power of attorney.
- **Source:** Bank of America, 2024 (as cited by InvestmentNews).
- **Fix:** a basic will and a list of your accounts, written down this year.

### 2. They talk about money with their children
- **Key fact:** in the 2024 Bank of America survey, 69% of wealthy parents with adult children had discussed family wealth plans with them, typically when the children were about 31.
- **Source:** Bank of America, 2024 (as cited by InvestmentNews).
- **Fix:** start earlier than 31. Small, honest conversations about money, from the teenage years.

### 1. They think in generations
- **Key fact:** this habit ties the video together. Wealth fades fast (93% of grandchildren of the top 1% didn't stay there; 72% of the top 1% fell out within a decade), so the families who last plan beyond their own lifetime: they avoid expensive debt, hold for the long term, write things down and teach the next generation.
- **Source:** Kalsi and Ward, NBER, 2025 (as above).
- **Fix:** ask one question before big money decisions: "Will this still look smart in twenty years?"

## Closing
"Old money" isn't really about money that's old. It's about habits that make money last: building your own base, avoiding expensive debt, spending on what matters, holding for the long term, and writing things down for the people who come next. Pick one habit from this list and start it this month. Which one are you starting with?
