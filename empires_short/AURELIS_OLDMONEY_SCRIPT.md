## Cold open (~15 s)

Picture a family with a fortune. A big house, servants, the best schools. Everyone assumes the money will be there forever.

Now fast-forward two generations.

Economists Priti Kalsi and Zachary Ward followed American families through census records from 1850 to 1940. Of the grandchildren of men in the top 1%, 93% did not reach the top 1% themselves.
`[SRC: Kalsi & Ward, "The Gilded Age and Beyond: The Persistence of Elite Wealth in American History," NBER working paper, 2025 (via InvestmentNews)]`

And it doesn't even take that long. In the same study, 72% of people in the top 1% had fallen out of it within ten years.

So "old money" mostly doesn't stay old. What do the families who keep it actually do differently? Here are nine habits, and the evidence behind each one.

## Open loops

Habit number three is one that even most wealthy people haven't fully taken care of. And stay to number one, because it ties everything together.

## Myth-check

Quick warning first. You've probably heard that 70% of wealthy families lose their money by the second generation, and 90% by the third. It's usually credited to a Williams Group study of 3,200 families. But family-wealth consultant James Grubman challenged it in a 2022 paper literally titled "There is no 70% rule."
`[SRC: Williams Group study and Grubman, "There is no 70% rule" (2022), both as cited by InvestmentNews]`

The real research, like that census study, still shows wealth fading. Just don't trust a neat round number until you know where it came from.

## 9. They don't wait for an inheritance

Maybe you've caught yourself thinking, "One day, family money will sort this out."

Here's the thing. A Federal Reserve analysis looked at how much of the top 10%'s wealth traces back to inheritances and gifts. Depending on the assumption about investment returns, it was about a quarter, or about half. Either way, a large share of wealth at the top was built, not handed down.
`[SRC: Federal Reserve, FEDS Notes, "How Does Intergenerational Wealth Transmission Affect Wealth Concentration?" (June 2018), Survey of Consumer Finances 2016 — 25% or 53%]`

The fix: plan as if no inheritance is coming. Build your own base first.

**Old money lesson:** money that outlasts you starts with money you built yourself.

## 8. They avoid expensive debt

You pay the minimum on your card, the balance barely moves, and you tell yourself you'll deal with it next month.

The Federal Reserve says that in the third quarter of 2026, the average interest rate on credit card accounts that were charged interest was 22.36%.
`[SRC: Federal Reserve, G.19 Consumer Credit release (November 2026)]`

The fix: if you carry a balance, paying it off is one of the highest "returns" you can get. Treat it like an emergency.

**Old money lesson:** debt at over 22% eats money before it ever gets the chance to outlast you.

## 7. They don't buy status

You've seen it. The new car in the driveway that says "we made it."

In the research behind *The Millionaire Next Door*, nearly 37% of the millionaires surveyed said they'd bought their most recent car used, not new.
`[SRC: Stanley & Danko, The Millionaire Next Door (1996)]`

The fix: spend on what you value, not on what other people will see.

**Old money lesson:** status is spent today. Wealth is what's still there tomorrow.

## 6. They keep a budget

It's the end of the month and you're scrolling your banking app, wondering where it all went.

Same book: more than half of those millionaires said they stuck to a budget.
`[SRC: Stanley & Danko, The Millionaire Next Door (1996)]`

The fix: a simple monthly plan beats a perfect one you never keep.

**Old money lesson:** you can't make money outlast you if you don't know where it goes.

## 5. They automate saving

You fully mean to save "whatever's left." And there's never anything left.

A study in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* looked at one large US company. When new employees had to sign up for the 401(k) themselves, 37% joined. Under automatic enrolment, it was about 86%.
`[SRC: Madrian & Shea, "The Power of Suggestion," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2001]`

Same people. The only thing that changed was the default.

The fix: make saving the default. Move money on payday, before you can spend it.

**Old money lesson:** the families that last don't rely on willpower. They rely on systems.

## 4. They hold, they don't chase

The market drops, the headlines scream, and you feel the urge to *do something*.

Morningstar found that over ten years, investors earned about 1.2 percentage points a year less than the funds they owned. That's roughly 15% of the funds' potential gains, lost mostly to buying and selling at the wrong times.
`[SRC: Morningstar, "Mind the Gap 2025" (August 2025)]`

The fix: pick a simple long-term plan and stop reacting to the news.

**Old money lesson:** money outlasts you when you give it time and leave it alone.

## 3. They write things down: a will and a plan

Be honest: do you have a will? If you don't, you're far from alone.

In a 2025 Caring.com and YouGov survey of more than 2,500 American adults, only 24% said they had one. The most common reason? They "just haven't gotten around to it." That was 43%.
`[SRC: Caring.com & YouGov, 2025 Wills and Estate Planning Survey]`

And this is the one I told you about. Even among the wealthy, a 2024 Bank of America survey of 1,007 high-net-worth people found 52% lacked a will, an advance healthcare directive or a durable power of attorney.
`[SRC: Bank of America, 2024 (as cited by InvestmentNews)]`

The fix: a basic will and a list of your accounts, written down this year.

**Old money lesson:** money can only outlast you if someone knows what to do with it.

## 2. They talk about money with their children

Think back. Did anyone actually sit you down and explain money?

In that same Bank of America survey, 69% of wealthy parents with adult children had discussed family wealth plans with them, typically when the children were about 31.
`[SRC: Bank of America, 2024 (as cited by InvestmentNews)]`

Thirty-one. That's a long time to figure it out alone.

The fix: start earlier. Small, honest conversations about money, from the teenage years.

**Old money lesson:** the next generation can't keep what they were never taught.

## 1. They think in generations

This is the one I told you to stay for, because it ties everything together.

Remember the start: 93% of the grandchildren of the top 1% didn't stay there, and 72% of the top 1% fell out within a decade.
`[SRC: Kalsi & Ward, NBER working paper, 2025 (via InvestmentNews)]`

So the families who last plan beyond their own lifetime. They avoid expensive debt, hold for the long term, write things down and teach the next generation. Every habit on this list is really this one.

The fix: before any big money decision, ask one question. "Will this still look smart in twenty years?"

**Old money lesson:** making money outlast you is the whole game.

"Old money" isn't really about money that's old. It's about habits that make money last: building your own base, avoiding expensive debt, spending on what matters, holding for the long term, and writing things down for the people who come next.
